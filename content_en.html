Transformers

When the word Transformer is mentioned in the context of Artificial Intelligence, it refers to a family of artificial neural network (ANN) architectures which are based on the multi-head attention mechanism. This mechanism gives all models that utilize it, the power to analyze large amounts of data and perform operations with all of it at the same time. Previous types of AI models, such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), could not work with such large amounts of data at the same time and more importantly, could not analyze the long range relationships between different “blocks” of data reliably. (Vaswani et al., 2017)

Transformer Architecture

Transformer models were initially designed to work with text data - as the evolution of RNN based sequence models created for machine translation. Nevertheless, as of today, they have proven to be a useful tool in almost every branch of machine learning, from computer vision and speech recognition to self driving and more.

Due to the transformer models’ ability to discern connections between data, they are extremely versatile and can be used in almost any machine learning field. This is why they currently are one of the most used types of AI models.

  • Chatbots and other smart assistants have become a part of everyday life.
  • Search engines have become more intuitive to use.
  • Machine translation has become more reliable.
  • Science & Medicine research has been greatly accelerated.
  • Many autonomous, previously unthinkable systems such as self driving vehicles, have become a reality

To put it simply, before transformers, AI was narrow, designed only for a single or a few select tasks and even then, it was sometimes unreliable. Today’s transformer based AI is general-purpose and is much more reliable than before.

In the next two chapters, I will attempt to outline how transformer models changed AI in terms of text and image based tasks.

Vision Transformers VS CNNs

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) were originally designed for image based tasks such as image generation and image recognition. CNNs work on the basis of mathematical convolution operations. These operations give the CNNs their vision, but this vision is fundamentally flawed.

CNN Locality Flaw

Take a look at this image. You can see the entire image at the same time. As a human, as long as you have two working eyes, you can immediately discern that this is the Lorax.

CNNs cannot “see” the entire image at once.

A CNN layer would do the following to this image: It would start scanning the image pixel by pixel, from top to bottom, left to right. Around each pixel, it would scan a predetermined area of 1 pixel for example. This way, it would only be able to “see” a 3x3 pixel grid (for example) at any given time. This 3x3 pixel grid is called a kernel.

Color images are made out of three monochrome layers: a red layer, a green layer and a blue layer.

CNN Interactive Showcase - Kernel

By applying this pixel to pixel scan to all three monochrome layers and analyzing the relations between each scanned kernel, it can create a single image, which is a 2D feature map. A CNN consists of multiple of these CNN layers with kernels which have different properties. By analyzing these images and creating different representations of the image data via the different kernels, the CNN can attempt to “understand” what it is “looking at”.

This way of viewing images is flawed, since it only takes into account small areas of an image to try to deduct what the subject of the image is. A simple analogy would be to try to deduct what a jigsaw puzzle will look like when it is finished by looking at three jigsaw pieces connected together. Depending on the size of the puzzle, this would range from very difficult to completely impossible.

This limitation leads to a severe side effect. CNN based AI will be more influenced by color rather than by shape. A CNN would classify the left image as a macaw and the right one as a cat. The two are the same image. The first image is simply more saturated. (Turc, 2025)

This limitation can only be mitigated, not fixed.

How would a Vision Transformer do it?

CNNs can only analyze small areas of an image and therefore understand only the relations between adjacent pixels. Thanks to their self-attention mechanisms, Vision Transformers (ViT) can analyze an entire image at once. This means that they can understand all relations between all pixels. A transformer model would scan the image pixel by pixel (the current scanned pixel in ViTs is called the query pixel), but instead of scanning a local area (3x3, 4x4, 5x5, etc.) around each pixel, it would scan the entire image around each pixel. Basically, it would scan the entire image as many times as there are pixels in the image. And for each scanned pixel, it would map out how the scanned pixel relates to all other pixels in the image.

Transformers can do Convolution

Taking the above into account - the principle between the two types of analysis is almost the same. Vision Transformers just do what CNNs do, but better, since they take more pixel relations in account. Therefore, Vision Transformer models can do anything CNNs can do. This is why there are almost no reasons left to use CNNs.*

* CNNs are still preferred for: Edge devices; Real-time systems; Smaller datasets; Lower computational cost

Performance

CNNs allow parallel computation within each layer but still process data through layers sequentially. Transformers, in contrast, enable parallel processing not only within layers but also across all input elements through self-attention, making them especially efficient for sequence modeling. This is why transformer models are faster than CNN based models.
